## 1. Overview, Team & Responsibility

**Project Repository:** https://github.com/planetai05/ML-Hackathon-PT-085

PathSnap takes a noisy 1 Hz GPS track and the road network around it, and outputs the sequence of road arcs the vehicle actually drove. The pipeline has four stages:

1. **Candidates:** for each GPS point, find the nearby road arcs (candidate generation).
2. **Classifier:** a classifier gives each (point, candidate arc) pair a probability of being the correct arc.
3. **HMM/Viterbi:** an HMM and Viterbi decoding turn those per-point probabilities into one connected route.
4. **Serving:** a FastAPI service on Laptop 1 runs the model. A Streamlit UI on Laptop 2 calls it over the LAN.

#### Q1. How did we preprocess the data?
1. **Parsing.** We loaded four files per trip:
   - `.track`: lon, lat, time
   - `.nodes`: lon, lat
   - `.arcs`: from-node, to-node
   - `.route`: ordered arc ids
2. **Projection.** The trips are spread worldwide. Longitudes range from -100.4° to 137.2° in train, and 23 of the 90 trips have negative longitude. A single fixed projection would therefore distort distances. Each trip is projected to the UTM zone of its own centroid (`features/trip_io.py::utm_epsg`), so every distance feature is in metres.
3. **Candidate generation** (`features/candidates.py`).
   - Each arc is sampled every 10 m into a KD-tree.
   - For every GPS point we keep the nearest arcs within a radius, capped at K arcs. Points with no hit get a second query at twice the radius.
   - The training feature tables were built with up to 15 arcs within 50 m (`docs/HANDOFF_PERSON_A.md`), giving on average 8.35 candidates per point.
4. **Labels** (`features/labels.py`).
   - The `.route` is not time-aligned with the GPS points. We therefore align it by dynamic programming: points move forward monotonically along the route, and the total point-to-arc distance is minimised.
   - A candidate gets `label = 1` if it is the aligned route arc for that point.
   - In some trips the GPS keeps recording after the route ends. Those points would get wrong all-zero labels, so a point is marked `label_valid` only if its aligned arc is within 50 m. Training and scoring use valid points only.
5. **Feature table.** For every (point, candidate) pair we compute 24 geometric and motion features (see Q2).

#### Q2. What patterns did we observe, and which features did we extract and why?
**Observed patterns** (partition in brackets):
- **The nearest arc is often wrong (train).** Near junctions and parallel roads, several arcs lie within GPS error, so distance alone cannot pick the correct arc. Motion features were needed.
- **Sampling is mostly 1 Hz with bursty gaps (both).** About 99% of steps are exactly 1 s. The rest are mostly 2–5 s (Figure 1), with rare multi-hour outages. Speed and heading features must therefore be computed from the real time difference, not by assuming 1 s.
- **Road networks are per-trip subgraphs (both).** Each trip has its own subgraph:
  - median 20 weakly connected components in train and 11 in student_test;
  - the largest component holds about 98% of the nodes;
  - arc ids are local to the trip.

  So the model cannot memorise arc ids. Features must describe geometry relative to the point.
- **Network size varies by orders of magnitude (both).** It ranges from about 1,500 to more than 300,000 arcs (Figure 1). Candidate search therefore needs a spatial index rather than a scan over all arcs.
- **Class imbalance (train).** Each valid point has at most one positive among its candidates. About 11.2% of train pairs are positive (`n_hits / n_candidate_rows` in `results/candidate_miss_rate.csv`).

**Features (24, in the order stored in `artifacts/model_metadata.json`), grouped by purpose:**
- **Distance:** `dist`, `dist_rank`, `dist_minus_min`, `dist_ratio`, `n_cand`. How close the arc is compared with the point's other candidates. Distance alone is not reliable, but its relative value is.
- **Position along the arc:** `proj_t`, `beyond_end`. Whether the point projects inside the arc or past one of its ends, which happens near junctions.
- **Arc size:** `arc_len`, `log_arc_len`. Short arcs near junctions are matched differently from long road sections.
- **Direction:** `heading_diff`, `cos_heading`, `heading_rank`, `heading_valid`. The angle between the GPS heading and the arc bearing. This separates opposite carriageways and crossing side streets. `heading_valid` flags points where the heading is unreliable, such as when stationary.
- **Motion and gaps:** `speed`, `accel`, `dt_prev`, `dt_next`, `dist_prev_pt`, `dist_next_pt`. Computed from the real time difference, because sampling is not always 1 s (see above).
- **Neighbour context:** `dist_ctx_mean`, `conn_prev_nearest`, `conn_next_nearest`. Whether the arc stays close to, and is connected to, the nearest arcs of the previous and next points. This gives the classifier short-range sequence context before the HMM.
- **Topology:** `out_deg_to`, `in_deg_from`. The node degrees at the arc's ends, which describe how ambiguous the local junction is.

## 3. Model Experimentation and Comparison

**Setup (Person B, `models/train_models.py`).**
- We trained seven models from six algorithm families on the same 24 features.
- Validation was **5-fold `GroupKFold` grouped by `trip_id`** on the 80 train trips, so no trip appears in both a training and a validation fold.
- Each model was then retrained on all 80 trips and evaluated once on the 10 `student_test` trips.
- All runs used CPU and `random_state=42`.

**Metrics.**
- *Macro F1* and *Log Loss* are computed on pairs (is this candidate the correct arc?).
- *Top-1* is computed on points: the share of valid points whose highest-probability candidate is the true arc. This is the "observation-level Top-1 accuracy" asked for in Q8.
- *Route continuity* is the share of consecutive classifier-only choices that are the same or connected arcs.

**Selection.** RF and XGBoost are tied within fold noise (CV Macro F1 0.9072 vs 0.9070, std ≈ 0.03). We selected **XGBoost** for four reasons:
- It is **4.6× faster at inference** than RF (0.87 vs 4.03 ms per 1k rows).
- It trains about 4× faster (20.0 s vs 79.1 s).
- Its artifact is about 0.4 MB, against roughly 34 MB for RF.
- The API scores every candidate of every point on each request, so inference speed matters.

### 3.2 Per-model Questions
For every model:
- *Training evidence* compares Top-1 on the training folds (`cv_train_top1`) with Top-1 on the held-out folds (`cv_top1`). Both come from `results/runs/<model>.json`.
- The loss column in the table shows the training objective.

#### 3.2.1 XGBoost (`xgb`): selected model
- **A1 – Why suitable:** the decision depends on interactions between features. For example, a 15 m distance is fine if the heading matches and suspicious if it does not. Boosted trees learn these interactions without manual feature crosses.
- **A2 – Strengths and limitations:**
  - Strengths: best or near-best on every metric; fast inference (0.87 ms per 1k rows); small artifact.
  - Limitations: it sees one pair at a time, so its route continuity is only 0.816. Sequence consistency has to come from the HMM.
- **B5 – Hyperparameters:** n_estimators=300, learning_rate=0.08, max_depth=6, subsample=0.8, colsample_bytree=0.8, tree_method=hist. Section 4 tunes these.
- **B6 – Loss:** binary logistic loss (`eval_metric=logloss`) with XGBoost's default L2 leaf regularisation.
- **C7 / C8 – Student-test results:** Top-1 **0.8677**, Macro F1 **0.9207**, Log Loss **0.1007**, precision 0.875, recall 0.855.
- **C9 – Over/underfitting:** mild overfitting. Training-fold Top-1 was 0.906 against 0.851 on held-out folds, and log loss was 0.074 against 0.122. Student-test Top-1 (0.868) is not lower than CV Top-1, so the model generalises to unseen trips. The gap shows it memorises some training-trip detail, which is why tuning searched regularisation parameters.

#### 3.2.2 Random Forest (`rf`)
- **A1 – Why suitable:** averaging many deep trees reduces variance across very different road layouts.
- **A2 – Strengths and limitations:**
  - Strengths: it ties for the best CV Macro F1 and has the lowest test log loss (0.1002).
  - Limitations: slowest training (79.1 s), slow inference (4.03 ms per 1k rows) and the largest artifact.
- **B5 – Hyperparameters:** 150 trees, max_depth=18, min_samples_leaf=5, max_samples=0.3. The depth limit and 30% bootstrap samples were chosen to keep memory and training time manageable.
- **B6 – Loss:** Gini impurity at each split. Probabilities are the averaged leaf frequencies.
- **C7 / C8:** Top-1 **0.8688**, Macro F1 **0.9213**, Log Loss **0.1002**.
- **C9:** mild overfitting, similar to XGBoost. Train Top-1 was 0.906 against 0.850 CV. Bootstrap subsampling and min_samples_leaf=5 kept the test score at CV level.

#### 3.2.3 LightGBM (`lgbm`)
- **A1 – Why suitable:** a boosting library built for large tabular data (1.7M pairs) that grows trees leaf by leaf.
- **A2 – Strengths and limitations:**
  - Strengths: accuracy close to XGBoost.
  - Limitations: the largest train-to-CV gap among the boosters, and slower inference than XGBoost (3.52 ms per 1k rows).
- **B5 – Hyperparameters:** n_estimators=400, learning_rate=0.05, num_leaves=63, subsample=0.8 (applied every round), colsample_bytree=0.8.
- **B6 – Loss:** binary log loss.
- **C7 / C8:** Top-1 **0.8670**, Macro F1 **0.9195**, Log Loss **0.1013**.
- **C9:** the most overfitting of the boosters. Train Top-1 was 0.913 against 0.848 CV, and log loss 0.068 against 0.124. 63 leaves is a lot of capacity for 24 features.

#### 3.2.4 HistGradientBoosting (`hgb`)
- **A1 – Why suitable:** scikit-learn's own histogram boosting, with no extra dependency. It is a useful check that the XGBoost result is not library-specific.
- **A2 – Strengths and limitations:**
  - Strengths: fastest of the boosters to train (7.3 s).
  - Limitations: slightly lower accuracy, and the highest test log loss of the boosters (0.1060).
- **B5 – Hyperparameters:** max_iter=300. Everything else uses the defaults: lr=0.1, max_leaf_nodes=31, and automatic early stopping on a 10% validation split.
- **B6 – Loss:** binary log loss.
- **C7 / C8:** Top-1 **0.8641**, Macro F1 **0.9180**, Log Loss **0.1060**.
- **C9:** the smallest gap of the boosters (train 0.891 against 0.848 CV). Together with its lower scores, this suggests slight underfitting from the 31-leaf cap and early stopping.

#### 3.2.5 Multi-layer Perceptron (`mlp`)
- **A1 – Why suitable:** a neural network can learn smooth non-linear combinations of the scaled features. It tests whether a different model family does better than trees.
- **A2 – Strengths and limitations:**
  - Strengths: fast inference (0.51 ms per 1k rows).
  - Limitations: it needs feature scaling, it was trained on a 600k-row subsample for time, and its accuracy is below the boosters.
- **B5 – Hyperparameters:** hidden_layer_sizes=(64, 32), Adam, batch_size=1024, max_iter=40, early stopping with n_iter_no_change=5, StandardScaler first.
- **B6 – Loss:** binary cross-entropy (log loss), minimised by Adam.
- **C7 / C8:** Top-1 **0.8656**, Macro F1 **0.9160**, Log Loss **0.1073**.
- **C9:** no overfitting. Train Top-1 was 0.886 against 0.846 CV. In Figure 3, training loss is still falling slowly while validation accuracy levels off after about epoch 24, and early stopping ends training at epoch 30. This is the only model with an epoch-wise history, so it is the only one with a training curve.

![Figure 3 – MLP training loss and validation accuracy per epoch (final fit), from results/runs/mlp.json.](results/figures/report/mlp_training_curve.png)

#### 3.2.6 Logistic Regression (`logreg`)
- **A1 – Why suitable:** a simple linear baseline. It shows how much of the problem distance and heading features solve on their own.
- **A2 – Strengths and limitations:**
  - Strengths: fastest inference (0.29 ms per 1k rows) and simple to interpret.
  - Limitations: it cannot model feature interactions, so its log loss is about 30% worse than XGBoost (0.1305 vs 0.1007).
- **B5 – Hyperparameters:** C=1.0, max_iter=500, StandardScaler first.
- **B6 – Loss:** L2-regularised log loss (lbfgs solver).
- **C7 / C8:** Top-1 **0.8578**, Macro F1 **0.9047**, Log Loss **0.1305**.
- **C9:** underfitting. It has the smallest train-to-CV gap (0.876 against 0.847) and the lowest training score of all models, so it lacks capacity rather than overfitting.

#### 3.2.7 k-Nearest Neighbours (`knn`)
- **A1 – Why suitable:** a non-parametric baseline that labels a pair by its most similar training pairs.
- **A2 – Strengths and limitations:**
  - Strengths: no training step (0.08 s).
  - Limitations: by far the slowest inference (156 ms per 1k rows, 19.9 s for the test set) and the worst log loss. That rules it out for the API.
- **B5 – Hyperparameters:** n_neighbors=25, weights='distance', StandardScaler, 200k-row training subsample.
- **B6 – Loss:** none is optimised. Predictions are distance-weighted neighbour votes.
- **C7 / C8:** Top-1 **0.8548**, Macro F1 **0.9055**, Log Loss **0.1558**.
- **C9:** overfitting. It has the largest train-to-CV gap (0.915 against 0.840). Distance weighting lets training points vote for themselves, and with 24 dimensions the neighbourhoods are sparse.

## 4. Bayesian Hyperparameter Optimisation (XGBoost)

Code: `hpo/optuna_search.py`. Results: `results/optuna_trials.csv`, `results/optuna_best.json`, `results/hpo_comparison.csv`.

1. **Hyperparameters searched:** n_estimators, learning_rate, max_depth, subsample, colsample_bytree, min_child_weight, reg_alpha, reg_lambda.
2. **Ranges and distributions:**
   - n_estimators: integer 150–450, step 50
   - learning_rate: log-uniform 0.02–0.20
   - max_depth: integer 4–9
   - subsample and colsample_bytree: 0.6–1.0, step 0.05
   - min_child_weight: integer 1–8
   - reg_alpha: log-uniform 0.001–5
   - reg_lambda: log-uniform 0.01–10
3. **Objective:** maximise mean observation-level **Top-1 accuracy** over **3-fold GroupKFold** on the train trips. For speed, each trial used a random 70% of the train trips (the seed depends on the trial number). Sampler: Optuna TPE. Pruner: MedianPruner, applied after each fold.
4. **Trials:** 30 in total, 22 completed and 8 pruned.
5. **Best values (trial 3, objective 0.8823):** n_estimators=300, learning_rate=0.1220, max_depth=5, subsample=0.80, colsample_bytree=0.85, min_child_weight=1, reg_alpha=0.1767, reg_lambda=0.0325.
6. **Did tuning help?** Only slightly, and not on accuracy. Both models were evaluated the same way: the same 5-fold GroupKFold on all 80 train trips, and the same 10 student-test trips.

| Configuration | CV Macro F1 | Student-test Top-1 | Student-test Macro F1 | Test Log Loss | Inference (ms per 1k rows) | Training time |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Untuned | **0.9070** | 0.8677 | **0.9207** | **0.1007** | 0.93 | 21.1 s |
| Tuned (Optuna) | 0.9068 | **0.8687** | 0.9192 | 0.1018 | **0.65** | **17.1 s** |

Student-test Top-1 rose by only 0.001, and CV Macro F1, test Macro F1 and log loss got slightly worse. All of these differences are far smaller than the fold-to-fold std (about 0.03). The real gains are efficiency:
- inference about 30% faster (shallower trees, max_depth 5 vs 6);
- training about 19% faster;
- artifact size 249 KB vs 464 KB.

The trial objective (0.882) is higher than the 5-fold CV Top-1 (0.850) because each trial scored a different 70% subset of trips with 3 folds. Trial values therefore rank configurations only roughly and are not comparable with the final CV. We deploy the tuned model (`artifacts/best_model_tuned.joblib`) because it is faster at the same accuracy.

## 5. HMM and Viterbi Route Reconstruction

Code: `hmm/viterbi.py`, called from `models/predictor.py`. Parameters: `artifacts/hmm_config.json`. Results: `results/hmm_comparison.csv`, produced by `scripts/evaluate_hmm.py` on the 10 student-test trips.

1. **Hidden states:** at time t, the states are the candidate arcs of GPS point t, the same candidate set the classifier scored.
2. **Emission probabilities:** the tuned XGBoost probability for (point t, arc), used in log space: log(max(p, 1e-6)).
3. **Transition probabilities:** fixed values based on network topology.
   - Same arc: 0.50.
   - Connected arc (the previous arc's end node is the next arc's start node): 0.45.
   - Any other arc: 0.001.

   Transitions use no distance or time term. Decoding is the standard log-space Viterbi recurrence with backpointers.
4. **Missing candidates and large GPS gaps:**
   - A point with no candidates is skipped, and the next point with candidates is linked to the last point that had them. Candidate generation retries at twice the radius first, so this did not happen on student-test (0 empty points).
   - Long time gaps are **not** treated specially. `hmm_config.json` defines `max_gap_seconds = 10`, but the current decoder does not use it.
5. **Disconnected consecutive segments:** if the best candidates at t and t+1 are not connected, the decoder can still jump. The transition costs log(0.001) ≈ -6.9 and is taken only when the emissions outweigh it. There is no shortest-path search to fill the missing arcs, so the decoded route can still contain jumps. This is why continuity is 0.977 and not 1.0.
6. **Did HMM decoding help?** Yes.

| Metric (10 student-test trips) | Classifier only | Classifier + HMM/Viterbi |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| Top-1, weighted by GPS points | 0.8687 | **0.8830** |
| Top-1, mean of per-trip values | 0.8848 | **0.8956** |
| Route continuity, mean of trips | 0.8172 | **0.9720** |
| Route F1 against reference (mean of trips) | – | 0.9461 |
| Trips where Top-1 improved | – | 7 of 10 |

The point-weighted figure counts every GPS point once. The per-trip mean gives a 262-point trip the same weight as a 3,958-point trip. We report both so that neither is mistaken for the other.

**Where HMM helped:** it fixed short zig-zags between parallel or crossing arcs. Continuity rose by 11–26 points on 8 of 10 trips, for example 00000076 (0.729 → 0.975) and 00000091 (0.713 → 0.967).

**Where it did not help:** Top-1 dropped on three trips:
- 00000007: -0.2 points
- 00000009: -1.1 points
- 00000052: -0.8 points

On these, the classifier was already accurate and the fixed "stay on the same arc" bonus held on to an arc a few points too long at junctions. Trip 00000077 remains the hardest (Top-1 0.734 even after HMM).

## 6. Geographical Visualisation Using Folium

## 7. API, UI and Deployment

### 7.1 Architecture
- **Laptop 1 (model host)** runs the FastAPI service (`api/main.py`, `scripts/run_api.ps1` / `.bat`) on `0.0.0.0:<port>`. It loads the Predictor (`PATHSNAP_PREDICTOR`, default `models.predictor:Predictor`) with the tuned XGBoost and HMM artifacts. It reads trip folders from `PATHSNAP_TRIPS_DIR`, and only it reads `.route` files, after prediction, to compute metrics.
- **Laptop 2 (client)** runs the Streamlit UI (`ui/app.py`, `scripts/run_ui.ps1` / `.bat`) with `PATHSNAP_API_URL=http://<laptop1-ip>:<port>`. It has no model or dataset and talks to the API only over HTTP/JSON.
- **Pluggable model.** Any class that follows the interface in `docs/API_CONTRACT.md` (`predict_trip(trip_dir)` and `score(pred, trip_dir)`) can be plugged in without changing the API or UI.

### 7.2 Endpoints (schemas in `api/schemas.py`)
| Endpoint | Purpose | Key fields / errors |
|---|---|---|
| `GET /health` | Readiness and model status | `service="pathsnap-api"`, `predictor_mode` (real / mock / unavailable), `model_name`, `n_trips` |
| `GET /trips` | List available trips | `trips`, `count`; 503 `trips_dir_missing` |
| `POST /predict` | Predict and score one trip | `predicted_arcs`, `predicted_route_geojson`, `per_point_arc`, `metrics`, `timings_ms`, `mode`; 404 `unknown_trip`, 422 validation, 500 `prediction_failed`, 503 `predictor_unavailable` / `trips_dir_missing` |
| `POST /evaluate-zip` + `GET /evaluate-zip/{id}`, `/results`, `/download` | Background job that runs every trip in a ZIP and returns per-trip metrics and a CSV | job status and progress, aggregate means, per-trip table |

**Safety and quality measures:**
- `trip_id` must match `^[A-Za-z0-9_-]{1,64}$`, which blocks path traversal.
- A mock predictor exists for UI development only. Its responses are labelled `mode="mock"` and are never shown as ML results.
- The UI checks `/health` for `service="pathsnap-api"`, so it cannot mistake another service on the same port for the API.
- Every prediction is logged on Laptop 1's console as `PREDICT trip=… mode=… model=… top1=… latency=…ms client=<ip>`.

### 7.3 Streamlit UI
Main elements:

## 9. Limitations and Future Work

1. **HMM transitions ignore distance and time.** Using shortest-path distance and the time difference between points in the transition score, and splitting the sequence at long gaps (`max_gap_seconds`), would reduce jumps and the Top-1 drops seen on 3 trips.
2. **Candidate settings.** The training tables used up to 15 candidates within 50 m. `features/candidates.py` defaults to 12 within 40 m with a 2× fallback. Using the same settings everywhere would remove a possible train/serve difference.
3. **Latency.** Viterbi takes most of the request time on long trips (up to about 3.6 s). A vectorised or windowed decoder would make long trips faster.
4. **Hardest trip.** Trip 00000077 (Top-1 0.734) needs per-case analysis of its dense parallel roads.
